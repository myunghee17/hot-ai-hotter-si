Marvell buys Lumentum for $115B in one of tech's biggest deals ever

$115B enterprise value 4.5 MRVL shares per LITE share Close targeted H1 2028

Marvell Technology announced November 2 a definitive agreement to acquire optical-components maker Lumentum in an all-stock transaction with an implied enterprise value of $115 billion — about $1,132 per Lumentum share, a 23.9% premium to its unaffected October 28 close. Lumentum shareholders will receive 4.500 Marvell shares per share; after closing, current Marvell holders will own about 67% of the combined company and Lumentum holders about 33%.

The combination unites Marvell's switching, custom silicon, SerDes and optical DSP technology with Lumentum's lasers, optical modules, optical circuit switching and compound-semiconductor manufacturing. The companies put the combined addressable market at $550 billion, project more than $1 billion in annual run-rate cost synergies, and expect the deal to add to Marvell's non-GAAP earnings per share in the first year after closing — all company projections, not results. Lumentum also reported fiscal Q1 revenue of $1.26 billion alongside the announcement.

Why it matters: this is a bet that AI scale-up is now an optics problem — moving data between accelerators with light, not copper, at rack and data-centre scale. The market's first reaction was sceptical: Marvell shares fell about 13% on November 2 and Lumentum about 19%, and the deal still needs both shareholder votes and regulatory approvals.

02  NVIDIA and Cisco unveil USFP, a 400Gb/s-per-lane optical form factor

NVIDIA and Cisco announced November 3 the Ultra-Dense Small Form Factor Pluggable (USFP), a high-density optical module designed to carry AI fabrics from 200Gb/s per lane to 400Gb/s per lane. Comparable in size to an OSFP module, USFP is designed to deliver four times the bandwidth and front-panel bandwidth density, with about 1.3 times the power efficiency of 200Gb/s-per-lane pluggable modules. The two companies are working with TE Connectivity and Amphenol on cages, connectors and modules, and plan an open industry specification through a community-led multi-source project.

Why it matters: as inference clusters grow, the bottleneck is moving data between racks without expanding the switch footprint or the power budget — and the two biggest AI networking names are now standardising the next lane speed together.

03  Qualcomm's near-memory AI silicon passes lab validation; Dragonfly AI250 samples in early 2027

Qualcomm announced November 2 that its first-generation High Bandwidth Compute (HBC) silicon has been validated in its San Diego lab, exercising the full memory read-write path and near-memory calculations at production-representative conditions using industry-standard automated test equipment. HBC stacks LPDDR dies on an active AI accelerator base die using wafer-to-wafer bonding, shrinking the distance data travels from millimetres to micrometres. Qualcomm claims up to 18 times LPDDR's memory bandwidth and more than five times HBM4's energy efficiency, measured in terabits per second per watt — company figures, based on measured silicon normalised to target conditions, not independent benchmarks.

Why it matters: this is a development milestone, not a product launch, but it gives Qualcomm working silicon behind its attack on the inference memory wall — its Dragonfly AI250 inference platform, a fully liquid-cooled rack with 38.6 TB of memory and a claimed 7.4 petabytes per second of effective bandwidth, is targeted to begin sampling in the first half of 2027.

06  Meta and Panmnesia propose running a whole data centre like a single chip

Meta and fabless semiconductor company Panmnesia jointly proposed November 2 a next-generation AI data-centre architecture, published as a review in Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering, in which an entire data centre operates in a single CXL-based coherence domain. Three hardware elements — a high-fan-out non-blocking switch, a link acceleration unit and a fabric controller — bound latency variability, and CXL-over-optics extends the fabric beyond the rack. Against a reference of one CPU coordinating two accelerators, the proposal raises that to sixteen accelerators per CPU and a coherence domain of up to 960 accelerators, with round-trip latency falling from microseconds to several hundred nanoseconds.

Why it matters: it is an architecture proposal, not shipping hardware, but it shows where hyperscaler thinking is headed — the unit of execution is moving from the chip to the rack to the whole building, and the interconnect standard that wins that domain will shape the next decade of system design.

Source: StorageNewsletter / Meta–Panmnesia review, Nov. 2, 2026 →

Cover Story · 01

Nvidia's shipping Rubin is slower on memory and NVLink than previewed — compute unchanged

ServeTheHome reported November 1 that Nvidia has quietly locked down lower shipping specifications for its Rubin GPU, just as the first Vera Rubin NVL72 systems reach general availability at hyperscalers. The revisions, posted to Nvidia's architecture datasheet in September and spotted by IT Home, cut HBM4 memory bandwidth from a preliminary 22 TB/s to 19.2 TB/s per GPU and NVLink scale-up bandwidth from 3.6 TB/s to 3.0 TB/s, while scale-out networking rose from 0.4 TB/s to 0.45 TB/s. Compute targets are untouched at 50 PFLOPS of NVFP4 inference and 35 PFLOPS of training, and memory capacity holds at 288 GB of HBM4 per GPU.

The rack-scale numbers move in lockstep: the Vera Rubin NVL72 drops from 1,580 TB/s to 1,400 TB/s of aggregate HBM4 bandwidth and from 260 TB/s to 216 TB/s of NVLink. The smaller HGX Rubin NVL8 takes the biggest hit — capacity down from 2.3 TB to 2.0 TB and bandwidth down 26%, from 176 TB/s to 130 TB/s, implying roughly 7 of each GPU's 8 HBM4 stacks are populated. Nvidia has said the earlier figures were preliminary targets and the new ones reflect qualified volume production; it has not said why the numbers moved. Even trimmed, Rubin's 9.375 Gbps/pin still sits above the HBM4 spec's 8 Gbps/pin and well above AMD's reported 7.6 Gbps/pin on MI455X — but buyers planning clusters around the original figures now have less memory and interconnect headroom than promised.

Cover Story · 01

Nvidia's Vera CPU holds its own against x86 in first independent benchmarks

Tom's Hardware published standalone benchmarks of Nvidia's 88-core Vera server CPU on October 30, testing a dual-socket MGX Vera reference platform against Intel Granite Rapids-AP and AMD EPYC Turin systems. Vera — built on Nvidia's custom Olympus cores for agentic AI workloads, where CPUs orchestrate tools, code execution, and data movement around GPUs — led on memory bandwidth and Python performance, edged a dual EPYC 9755 system by about 1.5% on SPEC CPU 2026 intrate, and stayed 8.6% ahead of a dual Xeon 6980P on generic GCC builds, while trailing in compression and cryptography workloads where x86 optimizations still dominate.

The result matters less as an x86 upset than as proof that Nvidia's full-platform play is credible: a CPU that feeds GPUs without becoming the bottleneck. Vera pairs its 88 cores (176 threads per socket) with up to 1.5 TB of LPDDR5X memory at roughly 1.2 TB/s of bandwidth and 1.8 TB/s NVLink-C2C links to GPUs, and Phoronix testing found near-linear scaling from one socket to two. The caveat is that these are local reference-platform tests, not cloud instances — Oracle's standalone Vera instances, generally available since October 27, remain unbenchmarked in production.
