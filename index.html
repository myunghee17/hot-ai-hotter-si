Antitrust Lawsuit Accuses Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, and Google of an Illegal AI Slowdown Pact

A new antitrust lawsuit filed Friday in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California alleges that Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google struck an illegal agreement to slow their respective AI development — cutting the value paying subscribers get from ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, and Gemini. The complaint centers on September 12, when Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a 3,800-word essay urging industrywide deceleration for safety, and Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and Demis Hassabis each publicly agreed the same day.

The suit also points to earlier groundwork: a July 2026 statement signed by senior employees at several labs acknowledging "intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow" and calling for government-backed global coordination. Amodei anticipated the antitrust risk in his essay, proposing a narrow government waiver for safety conversations; lead attorney Nick Rowley argues safety pacts by "for-profit technology companies" amount to "collective restraint for individual accountability." No lab has publicly responded yet.

01

Reasoning moves from training into the moment of use

Some systems spend more computation while answering: generating intermediate work, checking alternatives, or using tools before responding. This changes the old assumption that a model has one fixed cost and one fixed level of capability.

The useful question is not simply "Which model is smartest?" It is "How much time and compute does this task deserve?" Expect products to expose that trade-off more clearly.

Watch: latency × answer quality OpenAI
02

Multimodality becomes a foundation, not an add-on

Text, images, audio, and video are increasingly handled inside one model experience. The shift matters because information is not always translated into text first; timing, layout, and visual relationships can stay part of the task.

The test is coherence across modes — not whether a demo can label an image, but whether the system can follow a conversation that moves naturally between seeing, listening, and explaining.

Watch: cross-modal consistency Google
03

Smaller models find their own territory

Compact models can trade breadth for speed, cost, privacy, and control. On-device or tightly scoped systems are especially useful when a task is repetitive, the data is sensitive, or connectivity is unreliable.

This is not a simple race between small and large. The more durable pattern is a portfolio: local models handle routine work while larger systems take the difficult or ambiguous cases.

Watch: useful work per watt Google Developers
04

Agents leave the demo stage — and meet the messy world

An agent is more than a chatbot with a longer prompt. It must choose actions, operate tools, recover from errors, and know when to stop. Reliability depends as much on permissions and system design as on the model itself.

The strongest releases will make failure legible. Look for clear action previews, bounded access, human checkpoints, and a record of what the system actually did.

Watch: completion with correction Anthropic
05

Evaluation shifts from leaderboards to lived tasks

A benchmark score compresses many behaviors into one number. Real use is less tidy: a model may be excellent at retrieval and weak at instruction-following, or accurate in one language and unreliable in another.

The better comparison starts with a task, a failure budget, and representative examples. Public benchmarks remain useful signals, but they are the opening question — not the verdict.

Watch: task-level failure modes Anthropic

Three terms to keep straight

Context window
The amount of information a model can consider in one interaction — not a guarantee that every detail will be used equally well.
Inference
The work performed when a trained model generates an answer, including any extra reasoning or tool calls.
Agent
A system that uses a model to plan and take actions through tools, usually within defined permissions and checkpoints.
