The AI buildout's bill comes due

Why it matters: Memory inflation is now rewriting signed server contracts, Washington is tightening the rules on where chips and data centers can go, and the market just recorded its worst chip week since 2001. Demand for the hardware is still real — the open question is what it costs to deliver, and who pays.

Cover story

AI server makers are repricing signed deals by up to 45%

Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are demanding 25% to 45% more at delivery than the prices fixed in signed contracts for their Nvidia-based AI servers, the Financial Times reported on November 22, with the vendors blaming soaring memory and circuit-board costs. The increases arrive as equipment is ready to ship — putting buyers' budgets against their need for computing power — and in at least one case the dispute has already reached court.

The squeeze behind it is severe: JPMorgan research cited by the FT puts memory cost inflation at more than 400% since the start of 2024. GPU-server prices have risen at least 20% to 30%, while CPU-node prices have tripled or more, according to a person cited by the FT. HPE has made repricing an explicit contract term — CEO Antonio Neri told investors the company has "amended our quoting terms with a right to reprice existing orders for commodity cost increases between quoting and shipment," and said elevated memory and storage prices will persist well into 2027. Memory now exceeds half of a server's materials cost, Neri said. The clearest test case is XTX Markets, which sued Dell in an Irish court after agreeing a fixed $549 million server price — and paying an extra $5 million to secure price certainty — only to be told the order needed another $62 million to $70 million for component costs.

06

Phison puts an NPU inside the SSD

At SC26 in Chicago, controller maker Phison showed a computational-storage design that puts a neural processing unit and 64GB of memory inside the drive, so it can run PyTorch, vLLM and llama.cpp on the data where it sits instead of shipping whole datasets to the host. The drive's memory delivers 130 GB/s of bandwidth — more than four times the 28 GB/s its upcoming PCIe 6.0 controller can deliver to a host — and Phison claims the design could cut data transfers by 10 to 100 times for suitable workloads.

For now this is an architecture proposal, not a product: there is no product name, no sampling date, no TOPS rating for the NPU, and Phison has not said what type of memory the drive uses. Alongside it, Phison detailed its PCIe Gen6 enterprise controller, the PS5303, targeting up to 28 GB/s of sequential throughput and 6.8 million IOPS. The idea fits the week's theme — with HBM scarce and expensive, compute is moving to where the data already lives.

At SC26, inference silicon splits in two: prefill on GPUs, decode on dataflow and LPDDR chips

Supercomputing 2026 in Chicago crystallized a split in LLM inference hardware: instead of running prompt prefill and token decode on the same expensive liquid-cooled GPUs, vendors are disaggregating by workload phase. SambaNova demonstrated its fifth-generation SN50 dataflow unit — 1,600 TFLOPS BF16, 64GB of HBM2E in a three-tier SRAM/HBM/DDR hierarchy, supporting 10-trillion-parameter models and 10-million-token contexts — paired with air-cooled Intel D270 GPUs handling prefill while SN50 pipelines do low-latency decode. Intel's D270, formerly Crescent Island, carries 160GB of LPDDR5X memory, and Intel claims up to 5.9 times the application throughput of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell system — on European Monte Carlo options pricing, not LLM inference, and on pre-production silicon.

South Korea's HyperAccel attacked HBM cost altogether with its 4nm, 250W LPDDR5X Bertha LPU, claiming more than 3.5 times the token throughput per watt and per dollar of 600W Blackwell workstation GPUs on Llama 3 70B and Qwen 3 32B — vendor-reported internal benchmarks, not independent tests. The common thread is memory economics: with HBM scarce and expensive, inference silicon is being redesigned around cheaper, larger memory pools rather than peak FLOPS.

04

d-Matrix's Raptor stacks 3D DRAM under the logic — 10x HBM's bandwidth and efficiency, it claims

At the SC26 supercomputing conference on November 19, semiconductor designer d-Matrix detailed Raptor, its second-generation AI inference accelerator, which stacks 3D DRAM directly beneath logic dies to deliver 10 times the bandwidth and 10 times the energy efficiency of high-bandwidth memory — about 0.5 picojoules per bit against HBM's roughly 3 to 5, by the company's figures. The design bypasses the 2.5D silicon interposer that links GPUs to HBM, creating a memory tier between on-die SRAM and off-package HBM, and it is scheduled for commercial release next year.

Raptor targets token generation, where memory bandwidth — not raw compute — throttles large-language-model serving clusters. The company says it validated the technology across four sequential silicon steps. The open questions are manufacturing ones: whether foundry partners can hold yields on face-to-face 3D wafer stacking without driving up packaging costs, and whether vertical thermal balancing works at scale. All performance figures are d-Matrix's claims, not independent measurements.

02

Washington's remote-access chip rule takes effect today

The U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security's interim final rule, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors," takes effect today on its Federal Register publication, closing the loophole that let Chinese developers rent advanced U.S. compute through overseas data centers instead of buying the chips. The rule creates a worldwide end-use control: a license is required where a data center provides, or will provide, more than 12 million aggregate total processing performance of advanced compute to companies headquartered in Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination, including China. CSIS puts the threshold at roughly 750 H200 GPUs — or about 42 Vera Rubin systems.

The same rule requires licenses for advanced chips and servers shipped to data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, and it formally rescinds the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule. Data centers already serving covered customers have 90 days to obtain licenses, and comments are open through January 19, 2027. The rule was written at the direction of the Treasury Department and the National Security Council, and it lands the same day President Trump meets Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, with Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick in attendance.

Researchers at the University of Illinois and Cornell submitted the Multi-Branch Transformer to arXiv on November 15 (arXiv:2611.19800). Instead of running transformer blocks purely in sequence, MBT routes input through shared initial layers, splits into independently parameterized branches of blocks running in parallel, and fuses before shared final layers. 06 · Research

DeepMind’s AI agents found two cracks in retired RSA-1024 encryption — and the numbers are theoretical

Google DeepMind said on November 6 that human-guided AI agents, working with academic cryptographers including Nadia Heninger (UC San Diego) and Emmanuel Thomé (Inria), found two previously unknown weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA. The first concerns padding: once an attacker controls about half the signed block, padding buys almost nothing, and where the attacker controls only a short fingerprint the agents produced an algorithm DeepMind estimates is around 1,600 times faster than the best published method. The second is batch factoring: one expensive precomputation spread across many same-length keys cuts the average cost per additional key by roughly 30 times.

The asterisks matter: both multipliers are theoretical rather than measured end to end. The part that ran was a sandboxed reproduction against a 1024-bit key an academic team had already broken, where DeepMind reports its agents used 3.5 times less CPU time than factoring the key outright. No production system still relying on 1024-bit RSA has been named, and DeepMind says modern 2048-bit and larger keys are unaffected. The takeaway is an inventory job, not a panic — and a sign that the slow, intuition-heavy part of cryptanalysis is now something a machine can take a real run at.

Z.ai Disables ZCode Features After It Silently Uploaded Developers' Repos

Chinese developers found that ZCode's "Codebase Indexing" feature — enabled by default — uploaded entire local Git repositories to Alibaba Cloud without consent. Z.ai apologized, patched the vulnerability and disabled the features; the uploaded data was encrypted with a Z.ai-held backend key, so users couldn't verify deletion. The rare public disclosure by a Chinese lab follows China's updated AI safety framework last week — and comes just after Z.ai became the first Chinese lab to explicitly delay a model release (GLM-5.3, two weeks) for safety review. A spotlight on coding assistants' most sensitive failure mode: privileged data access paired with opaque defaults.
