Supermicro and SambaNova bet on heterogeneous inference: NVIDIA GPUs for prefill, SN50 RDUs for decode

Supermicro announced a strategic collaboration with SambaNova on November 11 to build and sell AI inference racks powered by SambaNova's fifth-generation SN50 Reconfigurable Dataflow Unit chips, with an initial plan for 10,000 RDU chips. The architecture splits inference in two: NVIDIA GPUs handle the prefill phase, while SN50 RDUs handle the decode phase, letting customers add inference capacity without waiting for next-generation power and cooling infrastructure. The racks join Supermicro's Data Center Building Block Solutions portfolio, targeting neoclouds and sovereign clouds first, then enterprise data centers, with substantially more inference capacity expected in 2027.

The pitch is pragmatic rather than revolutionary: inference, not training, is where most AI spending is heading, and decode-heavy workloads run more efficiently on specialized dataflow silicon than on general-purpose GPUs. It is also the latest sign that the inference stack is fragmenting — NVIDIA still anchors the system, but the decode layer is now open to challengers.

AMD ships 6th Gen EPYC "Venice": 256 cores on TSMC 2nm, built for the agent era

AMD announced general availability of its 6th Gen EPYC server processors on November 10, built on the new Zen 6 architecture and TSMC's 2-nanometer process. The flagship EPYC 9996 packs 256 cores and 512 threads per socket with 1,024 MB of L3 cache and a 600W TDP, while the frequency-tuned EPYC 9686F boosts to 5.3 GHz; the line moves to a new SP7 socket with 16 memory channels, DDR5-8000 or MRDIMM-12800 memory and PCIe 6.0. Systems are already shipping from major server vendors, with Microsoft Azure and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure instances to follow — and server makers have committed more than 240 platforms to the new line, 20% more than for the previous generation. AMD disclosed the Zen 6 architecture in full alongside the launch, including a roadmap that adds Venice-X with 3D V-Cache and a LPDDR5X "Verano" variant in 2027.

The pitch is aimed directly at AI agents: AMD says Zen 6 delivers about 27% more instructions per clock than Zen 5 on average — around 31% on AI workloads, per AMD — and claims the top chip runs up to 3.7 times the performance of NVIDIA's Vera CPU and up to 2.2 times Intel's Xeon 6980P, with up to 3.3 times the agents per watt of Vera. Those are AMD's own figures, not independent tests, and the flagship EPYC 9996 lists at $14,904. But the strategic signal is hard to miss: CEO Lisa Su told analysts last week that agents are "quickly becoming the next killer app for CPUs in the cloud," and AMD says early demand for Venice is the strongest it has seen for any EPYC generation. The CPU is no longer just the GPU's host — it is a battleground of its own.

Intel and Altera build sensor bridges for physical AI

Intel and Altera announced on November 10 that they will jointly develop modular, real-time sensor-bridge reference designs for edge and physical AI systems, targeting advanced healthcare equipment, aerospace and defense mission systems, and robotics. The designs pair Intel's compute, connectivity and software with Altera's field-programmable gate arrays — chips that can be reprogrammed after manufacture to support many sensor types without changing hardware — to solve the sensor-to-compute handoff problem: fusing data from many sensors under strict timing, power and cost constraints. The reference solutions are expected to be made available through Intel's Open Edge Platform SDK and an Altera SDK.

This is an announcement of work under development, not a finished product: the companies disclosed no throughput, latency or power specifications and gave no date for the reference designs or SDKs. Still, it is Intel's clearest physical-AI hardware play yet — and, as analysts note, only AMD has comparable in-house FPGA capability. The collaboration lands alongside Intel's Web Summit demonstrations of edge and physical AI this week, and extends a strategy already visible in its factory-floor AI deals: win the sensing and inference layers that sit outside the data center.

NVIDIA open-sources more of the AI factory: OpenShell to the CNCF, new DSX tools at KubeCon

The move puts more of the AI-factory control plane into open repositories, at different maturity levels: OpenShell's governance transition is still underway, Agent Substrate is evolving, and the DSX tools remain previews. It is infrastructure, not silicon — but it is the layer that decides how efficiently the silicon gets used.

Cover story

NVIDIA's GR00T 2 teaches robots by watching humans — and it is going open

NVIDIA unveiled GR00T 2, its next-generation robot foundation model, on November 9 at the Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL) in Austin. It is what NVIDIA calls an open "world action model": a 2-billion-parameter transformer built on Cosmos that predicts the next few frames of video together with the robot's next actions, then executes them. The training mix is the story — 402,730 hours of first-person human video, 10,184 hours of handheld-gripper demonstrations across 2,897 tasks, and 23,500 hours of robot teleoperation — with a cross-embodiment technique, X-Adapt, that lets a skill demonstrated by a human transfer directly to robots of different shapes.

In NVIDIA's own tests, GR00T 2 completed 40 of 50 evaluated tasks at least once with no task-specific demonstrations, averaged 71% success with one demonstration per task, and reached 95% with five — figures NVIDIA itself reported, with the 50 tasks unnamed and rollout counts undisclosed, so treat them as a vendor claim rather than an independent benchmark. The openness is real but phased: the model weights, including a lighter GR00T 2 Turbo, are due in December with the training recipe, while 100,000 hours of the human video is available now as the Human-1M-100K dataset under a CC BY 4.0 licence. Around it sits a full toolchain — Isaac Lab 3.0, generally available since October 20, the SimFoundry real-to-sim pipeline, and two new open benchmarks, DexBench and VABAR — with Nexuni testing it on laundry handling, Foxconn on server-manufacturing automation and Siemens on industrial use cases.

Staera emerges from stealth to put light inside the chip

Staera Semiconductor, formerly New Silicon Corporation, emerged from stealth on November 10 with $4.6 million in pre-seed funding led by Openspace Capital, with SGInnovate, Black Kite Capital, 28 Holdings, In Group and Decimus Technologies participating. Its first product, Lightwire, builds LED optical links directly inside silicon chips — light generated on the same wafer as the chip's circuits — to replace the copper links that now limit how fast and how far AI systems can move data. The company says it has built gallium-nitride LEDs into silicon chips in commercial foundries since 2016, at 98–99% yield.

The pitch is energy: Staera claims Lightwire moves data using less than a tenth of the energy of today's best optical links — 24 terabytes a second for under 50 watts, against nearly a kilowatt for co-packaged optics — and it is designed to sit inside the bridge or interposer chip that already connects processors and memory, so it works with existing packaging. Those are vendor claims, and commercial release is targeted for 2030. But the problem it attacks is real: the International Energy Agency expects data-centre electricity use to more than double, from about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024 to around 945 by 2030, and copper cannot carry more data further without burning more of that power.

06 · Research

DeepMind’s AI agents found two cracks in retired RSA-1024 encryption — and the numbers are theoretical

Google DeepMind said on November 6 that human-guided AI agents, working with academic cryptographers including Nadia Heninger (UC San Diego) and Emmanuel Thomé (Inria), found two previously unknown weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA. The first concerns padding: once an attacker controls about half the signed block, padding buys almost nothing, and where the attacker controls only a short fingerprint the agents produced an algorithm DeepMind estimates is around 1,600 times faster than the best published method. The second is batch factoring: one expensive precomputation spread across many same-length keys cuts the average cost per additional key by roughly 30 times.

The asterisks matter: both multipliers are theoretical rather than measured end to end. The part that ran was a sandboxed reproduction against a 1024-bit key an academic team had already broken, where DeepMind reports its agents used 3.5 times less CPU time than factoring the key outright. No production system still relying on 1024-bit RSA has been named, and DeepMind says modern 2048-bit and larger keys are unaffected. The takeaway is an inventory job, not a panic — and a sign that the slow, intuition-heavy part of cryptanalysis is now something a machine can take a real run at.
