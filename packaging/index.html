Cover story · CoWoS

TSMC's CoWoS Yield and Cost Strain Gives Intel an Opening, Analysts Say

CoWoS interposer = cost, yield & capacity bottleneck Intel EMIB in production; ZAM & XBM on roadmap One bad interposer can scrap a GPU plus 8 HBM stacks

TSMC's difficulty stitching AI accelerators together with high-bandwidth memory on a single package is giving Intel a real chance to win foundry customers, Counterpoint Research co-founder Neil Shah and other analysts told EE Times. TSMC's CoWoS technology — the 2.5D workhorse behind AI chips from Nvidia, AMD, and Broadcom — has become a production bottleneck: HBM's reliance on large CoWoS silicon interposers "carries steep costs with thick packages, yield risk, capacity constraints and high scrap costs, thus creating an opening for credible alternatives," Shah said.

01CoWoS Source · EE Times

The economics

The economics are brutal at the final step: large silicon dies already have high defect rates, and if the interposer fails during final packaging, the GPU die and all eight or more attached HBM stacks are ruined — "scrapping an assembled package because of an interconnect or interposer defect destroys thousands of dollars in silicon." Intel's EMIB bridge technology is already in production, and its longer-term Z-angle memory (ZAM) and cross-batch memory (XBM) concepts aim to eliminate the interposer problem entirely — a credible pitch to AI chipmakers hunting for a second source.

Regional buildout · India

Nexperia Partners with Tata Electronics on Chip Production and Packaging in India

Dutch chipmaker Nexperia said on Thursday it will partner with Tata Electronics, a subsidiary of the Tata Group, to produce and package chips in India. Nexperia — owned, but not controlled, by China's Wingtech following a dispute last year — is the latest global chipmaker to bet on India's push to build a domestic semiconductor supply chain.

Packaging is the natural beachhead for India's chip ambitions: assembly and test is less capital-intensive than leading-edge fabs but still anchors the supply chain and builds local expertise. If the Tata partnership scales, India moves from packaging aspirant to a real node in the global back-end map.

Regional buildout · United States

SK Hynix in Exploratory Talks with Intel on U.S. Memory Manufacturing

Apple memory supplier SK Hynix is in exploratory talks with Intel about manufacturing memory chips in the United States for the first time, according to Reuters. Options on the table include the South Korean chipmaker leasing part of Intel's planned manufacturing complex in Ohio, or a joint venture with Intel and major cloud providers looking to lock in memory supply. No decisions have been made.

The packaging angle matters here: SK Hynix already has an advanced packaging facility under construction in West Lafayette, Indiana, which will stack and package DRAM wafers shipped from Korea into HBM for U.S. customers. Pairing domestic front-end memory output with domestic HBM packaging would close one of the biggest remaining holes in the U.S. AI supply chain.

CoWoS, Wafer-Scale, and CoWoP: The AI Packaging Bottleneck Is Moving

An EDN analysis lays out how the industry's three architectural bets — CoWoS-style 2.5D integration, wafer-scale integration, and chip-on-wafer-on-PCB (CoWoP) — each move the packaging bottleneck somewhere new. CoWoS remains the proven production path for AI accelerators, but as packages grow, the humble package substrate becomes the stress point: escape routing, power-delivery networks, coefficient-of-thermal-expansion transitions, warpage management, and board-attach reliability all get harder with size.

The takeaway for packaging watchers: scaling AI compute is no longer just about the interposer or the die — it's about the entire "realization corridor" from die to board to system. Whichever architecture wins, substrate and panel-level innovation will decide how far multi-die packages can scale.

Market · Forecast

High-End Packaging Market Seen Nearly Doubling to $64.4B by 2030

The Business Research Company's latest report puts the high-end semiconductor packaging market at $36.55 billion in 2025, growing to $41.06 billion in 2026 — a 12.3% compound annual growth rate — and reaching $64.41 billion by 2030 at an 11.9% CAGR. The report credits demand for compact, high-performance chips, the limits of traditional PCB-based packaging, and the wider adoption of flip-chip and advanced interconnect technologies.

Numbers like these explain the capex race: when the packaging market itself is growing at ~12% a year, every foundry, OSAT, and materials supplier has to expand or fall behind. It also quantifies the stakes behind this week's CoWoS-vs-alternatives debate — the winner takes a slice of a $64 billion pie.

EMIB, decoded

Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge: Intel's approach to linking dies through small silicon bridges embedded in the package substrate.

High-bandwidth memory: vertically stacked DRAM positioned close to accelerators to deliver far more data bandwidth than conventional memory.
